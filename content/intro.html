THE PROBLEM

Some meters record less electricity than the home really uses.

A common way to steal power is to rewire around the meter, a “bypass”, so it only sees part of the load. Think of a hidden pipe running around a water meter: water still flows, the bill just stops counting it.

The utility loses revenue
Electricity is supplied but never billed, and honest customers end up carrying the cost.
Inspectors are scarce
Each area can visit only a handful of homes a week, out of hundreds or thousands of meters.
So the product is: where to look
A short, ranked list of homes that are worth a visit this week, each with a reason anyone can check.
WHO USES IT

Three people, one weekly list.

Superintending Engineer
Oversees a whole region. Receives the week’s list for each area and decides where inspection effort goes.
Assistant Engineer
Runs one local office area, a “sub-division”. Turns the list into this week’s field visits.
Field inspector
Visits the home and checks the wiring. Only the inspector decides whether theft happened; the tool never does.
HOW IT DECIDES

Three simple ideas, applied to every meter.

  1. 1
    Compare each home with its own past
    Its usual daily use over three months (“before”) against its use over the last month (“now”). A month in between is skipped, so a theft that starts slowly cannot hide in the “before” figure.
  2. 2
    Compare it with its neighbours
    Homes on the same street transformer live through the same weather and festivals. When autumn comes, everyone uses less air-conditioning, so a big drop on its own means nothing. A drop far bigger than the neighbours’ is what stands out.
  3. 3
    Rule out innocent explanations
    Meters that dropped for a good reason are set aside, and the reason is recorded:
    Rooftop solar Empty house Meter not reporting New or replaced meter Disconnected Drop too small to matter
{THRESHOLD} pts
The flag rule
A home is flagged when its use fell more than {THRESHOLD} percentage points further than its neighbours’, and by at least {FLOOR} units a day. Both limits are settings the utility can tune during a pilot, without changing code.

GLOSSARY

Six words you will meet in the demo.

Smart meter
A meter that records a home’s electricity use every 30 minutes and sends it to the utility automatically.
kWh (unit)
The unit electricity is billed in, often just called a “unit”. A typical home uses a few to a few dozen a day.
Transformer (DT)
The box on a pole that a street’s homes are connected to. Homes on the same transformer are treated as neighbours.
Feeder
A power line that carries electricity into an area and supplies several transformers.
Sub-division
A utility’s local office area. The inspection list is made for one sub-division at a time.
SE and AE
Superintending Engineer, who oversees a region, and Assistant Engineer, who runs one sub-division.
